Milo, age 6 · loves baseball, sports, and a good bedtime mystery

The Toy Chest League

The lid of the old wooden toy chest creaked open at the corner of Milo's room, just a crack, just enough for a small orange glow to spill out onto the carpet. Milo was still awake, lying on his side, watching. He blinked. The glow got brighter, and out popped a tiny baseball, rolling across the floorboards like it had somewhere important to be. "Wait," Milo whispered, sitting up. "You're not supposed to move."

The baseball wobbled to a stop at the foot of his bed. Its stitches glowed faintly red, like laces made of tiny lanterns. "There's a game tonight," the baseball said, in a voice like a bat cracking softly against a glove. "The Toy Chest League Championship. But we've got a problem." Milo climbed down onto the rug, careful and quiet so he wouldn't wake Nora, whose bed was just across the room. The toy chest lid swung all the way open now, and inside, in the soft glow, tiny toy figures were lining up on a felt-green field that hadn't been there before. A plastic shortstop. A stuffed-bear catcher. A whole miniature stadium made of blocks for walls and a night-light for a moon. "What's the problem?" Milo asked. "Nobody will say who won the last inning," said the baseball. "The bear says he tagged the runner out at second. The shortstop says the bear missed by a mile. Now everybody's arguing and the game can't finish." Milo crouched down at the edge of the chest so his eye was level with the tiny field. The bear catcher had his stuffed paws crossed. The shortstop, a small green army figure, stood with his little painted eyebrows scrunched down. "I definitely tagged him," the bear catcher said. "You totally didn't," said the shortstop. "You were still reaching when I touched the bag." Milo looked back and forth between them. He wanted to just say the bear was right, because the bear catcher looked so sure and a little sad, like he needed someone on his side. It would be easy. Nobody would know the difference. But Milo had actually been watching, hadn't he? He closed his eyes and pictured it — the tiny runner sliding, the bear's paw stretching, the dust of felt fibers puffing up. "I saw it," Milo said slowly. "The bear's glove got there right when the foot touched the bag. It was really, really close. I think it's a tie." The shortstop's eyebrows relaxed. The bear catcher uncrossed his paws. "A tie doesn't finish the game though," said the baseball, rolling in a thoughtful circle. "Then let's replay it," Milo said. "One more time, and everybody watches together instead of just guessing." The tiny players agreed. Milo lay flat on his stomach, chin propped on his hands, and watched the little runner take off toward second base again. This time, every player on the field leaned in to see. The bear's paw came down. The shortstop's foot touched the bag. It was close, so close, but Milo saw it clearly this time — the paw landed just a hair sooner. "Out," Milo said gently. "But barely. You were both amazing." The shortstop grinned. The bear catcher didn't look sad at all — he threw his stuffed arms up like he'd won something better than the play itself, and the whole tiny stadium lit up with soft cheering, little clapping sounds like popcorn popping far away. "Good call," the baseball said, rolling over to bump gently against Milo's hand. "That's why we asked you. You always tell it like you actually saw it." Milo smiled, suddenly noticing how heavy his eyelids had gotten. The tiny stadium lights dimmed themselves down, one by one, like stars going to sleep. The players folded down into the corners of the chest, the felt field rolled itself up small, and the baseball gave one last happy wobble before the lid creaked quietly shut. Milo climbed back into bed, pulling his blanket up to his chin. Across the room, Nora murmured something in her sleep and turned over. The room was dark and still, and the toy chest sat closed and quiet in the corner, keeping its little glow tucked away until tomorrow night. Milo closed his eyes, warm and cozy, and drifted off to sleep. The end. Sweet dreams, Milo.
Ozzie, age 3 · loves treehouses, stuffed animals, and stargazing

The Treehouse and the Waiting Star

High up in the old oak tree, the treehouse glowed with one small lamp. Wes and Ozzie climbed the last ladder step, and Scout waited below, tail thumping softly on the grass. Last night's fort was still folded up at home, but tonight the treehouse walls were hung with soft blankets, and a round window looked out at the sky. "Time for sleep, little one," Mom whispered, tucking a blanket around Ozzie's toes. Ozzie held his stuffed rabbit, Pockets, close. Pockets's ears flopped over, soft and friendly against the lamp light. Wes peeked out the round window. "Look! A star!"

But the star was hiding behind a little gray cloud. It blinked once, then went dark, then blinked again. "It's playing peekaboo," Wes said. Ozzie held up Pockets to the window. "Pockets wants to see too." They watched. The cloud moved slow, slower than a snail, slower than Scout after a big walk. Wes almost said *come on, cloud* out loud, but instead he just watched, and Ozzie watched too, and Pockets watched with his little floppy ears. Dad climbed up with two cups of warm milk. "What are you two looking at?" "The star is hiding," Ozzie said. "Stars do that sometimes," Dad said, sitting down between them. "Let's wait and see." So they waited. Wes held Pockets's paw up to the glass, like Pockets was waving to the sky. The cloud drifted a little more, then a little more, until finally it slid away completely, and the star shone bright and clear through the round window. "There it is!" Ozzie clapped. "You waited for it," Dad said, smiling. "Pockets helped," Ozzie said, hugging the rabbit close. "Soft. So soft." Ozzie patted Pockets's velvet ears against his cheek. Scout climbed the last few steps and curled up right in the middle of the treehouse floor, letting out a long, happy sigh. "Soft. So soft," Wes echoed, patting Scout's fur. Mom peeked her head up through the treehouse door. "Almost time to close your eyes." Ozzie yawned and set Pockets on the little shelf by the window, right where the starlight could reach him. "Do you think Pockets will dream about the star?" Wes asked, snuggling into his blanket. "Maybe," Mom said softly. "What do you think Pockets dreams about, way up in a treehouse?" Ozzie's eyes were already closing. "Star dreams," he murmured. "Soft. So soft," he whispered one more time, and then his breathing went slow and even. Wes looked out at the bright little star, then at Pockets resting on the shelf, then at Scout sleeping warm beside them. He pulled his blanket up to his chin. "Goodnight, Wes," Mom whispered. "Goodnight," Wes whispered back, and closed his eyes. The end. Sweet dreams, Wes, Ozzie.
Finn, age 8 · loves fishing, airplanes, and Grandpa's tall tales

The Runway by the River

Grandpa's old barn had turned into the best rainy-day place in the whole world. Tonight, though, the sky was clear and soft with the kind of orange light that comes just before bedtime, and Cole and Finn were sprawled on the porch with Grandpa while Grandma hummed in the kitchen and something warm and cinnamon-smelling drifted through the screen door. "Grandpa," Cole said, bouncing a little on the wooden step, "did you really see a real dinosaur bone at the air show?" Grandpa chuckled and rubbed his chin. "Well now. I said I saw something that *looked* like a dinosaur bone." Finn looked up from the fishing lure he was fiddling with, one eyebrow raised. "That's not what you said before. Before you said it *was* one."

Grandpa's ears turned a little pink. "Did I say that?" "You did," Finn said, but he said it kindly, the way you'd remind someone they left their jacket somewhere. Cole's face fell just slightly, like a balloon losing a bit of air. He loved that story. He'd told it to his whole class — a giant fossil, right there next to the airplane hangar, practically a triceratops rib. Dad, who was fixing a squeaky porch swing chain nearby, looked over. "Let's go see it," he said. "The hangar's not far. If it's really there, we'll all get to see a dinosaur bone. If it's something else, we'll figure out what." Mom was already getting her shoes, and Grandma poked her head out the door, wooden spoon still in hand. "Oh, I remember that thing. Bring a flashlight." So off they went, the whole crew, down the gravel road toward the little grass airfield where Grandpa kept his old biplane humming and happy in its hangar. Finn grabbed his tackle box for no real reason except that it made him feel ready for anything, and Cole ran ahead, arms out like wings, doing loop-the-loops in the cooling evening air. At the hangar, propped in the corner near a stack of tires, was the "bone" — long, curved, pale in the flashlight beam. Everyone leaned in close. "It's a boat rib," Dad said after a moment, turning it over. "See the notches? Somebody built this into an old rowboat once." Cole touched it. It really did look like something ancient. "It's still cool," he decided. Grandpa sighed, but it was a comfortable sigh, the kind that comes with a smile tucked inside it. "You're right. It's a boat piece, not a dinosaur bone. I got excited telling the story and it grew a little bigger every time, like a fish on Finn's line." Finn grinned. "My fish don't grow bigger. I just remember them accurately." "Sure you do," said Mom, laughing. Grandpa knelt down so he was closer to Cole's height. "Tell you what. Next week, there's a real dig happening two towns over — actual dinosaur fossils, the genuine article. I'll take you both, and this time I promise I won't stretch the story, even a little." Cole's eyes went wide and bright, all the disappointment gone. "Can we bring the airplane? Like, fly there?" "We can absolutely fly there," Grandpa said. "Weather permitting." Finn held up the boat rib, admiring it in the flashlight glow. "Can I keep this? It'd make a good handle for a net." "Take it," Grandpa said. "Least I can do." They walked back together under a sky gone the color of a plum, crickets starting up their evening song. Grandma had cocoa waiting, and cookies just cool enough to eat, and somebody had turned on the soft porch light that always made the moths dance politely at a distance. Cole curled up on the porch swing between Mom and Dad, the boat-rib story already turning, in his mind, into a good one instead of a lost one. Finn sat close by, turning his new fishing-net handle over in his hands, humming some tune only he seemed to know all the words to. The cocoa went down warm. The swing creaked its familiar creak. And one by one, eyes grew heavy, full of cinnamon air and cricket songs and the comfortable, settled feeling of a day that had ended exactly right. The end. Sweet dreams, Cole and Finn.

Why Bethae

Bedtime is one of the last unhurried moments in most families' day — and one of the easiest to lose to a tired parent, a repeated book, or a phone. Bethae exists to protect that moment, not replace it.

Every night, we write a brand-new story built around your child specifically — their name, their current obsessions, the things that make them laugh — instead of a template with a name dropped in. It shows up in your inbox, ready to read together, no app to open and nothing to remember.

Questions parents ask

Is this actually AI, and is it safe for my kid?

Yes — every story is written by AI, personalized to the profile you give us. Before anything reaches your inbox, it passes through an automated safety and quality review built specifically for bedtime reading — checking length, tone, and content. If a story doesn't pass, it's held back and regenerated rather than sent.

What happens to my child's information?

We only use it to write your child's stories. It's never sold, and it's not used to train any public AI model. You can update your family's information anytime from your account.

Can I pause or cancel?

Yes, anytime, right from your account — no call or email required. Pausing doesn't lose your story history; it just stops new stories until you turn it back on.

What does it cost?

It's free to join right now — no credit card required. When we introduce paid plans, everyone already signed up will get advance notice before anything changes.

How many kids can I sign up?

Up to four children per family in one signup, each with their own personalized profile and story.

What if my child doesn't like a story?

You can request a re-roll — a fresh story for that night — right from your account, up to twice a week per family.
